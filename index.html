About

The same instinct,
every chapter.

I've built my career around work that asks for both imagination and rigor: seeing the possibility before the pieces are in place, earning trust across very different stakeholders, and carrying the idea all the way through delivery. That instinct made me Vans Warped Tour's first intern after being told the tour didn't take interns. It led me to My Chemical Romance, and eventually to two missions I believed in enough to give more than eight years to each.

The settings changed, the instinct didn't. I connect things most people treat as separate until the path forward looks obvious, then build it: finding the leverage, aligning the right people, and making something that didn't exist before. Something people feel.

Much of the work I'm proudest of was built without the budget most people would insist on. I stopped treating limited resources as an obstacle a long time ago. At this point it's just how I think.

I'm rarely building for just the immediate outcome. The best work delivers in the moment, earns the trust that makes the next thing possible, and leaves behind a stronger way of working. I've done this inside organizations that operate at real scale — big audiences, distributed teams, real stakes — which is why I've never treated strategy and execution as separate skills. At that level, they're the same job.

Underneath all of it, the work has to feel true to the people it is for. If it involves a child, a service member, a fan, a community, or a senior stakeholder, they are never a device. They are the point. That is what turns strategy into something people remember.

Organizational Strategy | Executive Brief

USO Strategic Framework & Executive One-Sheet

I saw a larger future for an organization I believed in, so I put it on paper. I developed a 14-page enterprise strategy for positioning the USO as America’s nonprofit, then distilled it into a one-page executive brief. The framework made visibility, storytelling, and cultural engagement part of mission delivery. It sequenced awareness before affinity and giving, and reimagined entertainment, sports, and transitions around scale, trust, and distinctive value. I sent both to the incoming CEO because loyalty to an organization includes offering your clearest thinking, even when no one has asked for it.

Partnership Concept | Strategic Pivot

PRESSURE × Dunkin’ × USO

A three-way brand integration anchored to Memorial Day and the film’s D-Day narrative. The 72¢ donation mirrored the film’s 72-hour theme, spanning a limited-edition Dunkin’ cup, talent appearances, and service-member screenings. The exchange gave the studio a cultural story when traditional media was unavailable, Dunkin’ an authentic role in a patriotic moment, the USO awareness and a fundraising path, and Americans an easy way to support those who serve.

When world events made talent-led military promotion impossible, I moved the story to an overlooked collection of World War II letters. I brought in Legacybox to digitize them at no cost, then built a public archive around their human story of D-Day and a studio-funded mission immersion at Fort Campbell for 14 media and creators.

Food Network | Series Finale

The Great Food Truck Race | Finale Feature

A relationship I refused to treat as one-time.

After one event with Tyler Florence, I saw both his genuine connection to the military, and the larger untapped opportunity — The Great Food Truck Race. When my team declined another engagement, I found a cost-neutral path through the marketing team, and joined him at Fleet Week. I raised the show early, then let the experience do the rest. By dinner, Tyler was asking what we could do next, and suggested the series himself. I secured the producer introduction, wrote the creative brief, and pitched the concept. They made it the season finale. The placement helped the USO recognize the full value of the relationship, and Tyler later became a Global Ambassador.
